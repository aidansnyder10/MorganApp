Your cycle, start to finish

Hormonal birth control, copper IUDs, breastfeeding, PCOS, and stress can all change bleeding, cramps, cervical mucus, and whether you ovulate. Use Morgan as orientation—your clinician has the clinical detail.

Natural menstrual cycle (no hormonal suppression)

In a textbook 28-day cycle, ovulation often lands around mid-cycle—but “normal” cycle length spans roughly 21–35 days, and ovulation doesn’t arrive on clockwork for everyone. The “fertile window” usually includes the few days before and the day of ovulation; sperm can survive for several days in the body.

If you’re tracking for pregnancy prevention without hormones, fertile-window methods (symptothermal charting, monitors, clinician-taught FABM) outperform calendar guessing. Apps that only count days estimate risk poorly compared to basal body temps, LH tests, cervical mucus, or clinician guidance.

If you’re trying to conceive, timing intercourse or insemination around ovulation raises odds; fertility evaluation is recommended after 12 months under 35 (six months at 35+), or sooner if periods are irregular or you have other concerns.

Hormonal birth control & typical patterns

Combined oral contraceptives, the patch, many vaginal rings, and other regimens deliberately stabilize hormones to block ovulation reliably when taken as directed. Withdrawal bleeding on placebo week can look like a period without being identical to menstruation physiologically—wording differs by formulation.

Progestin-only methods (many pills, hormonal IUDs, injections, implants) may or may not halt ovulation; they thicken cervical mucus and thin the uterine lining, which still lowers pregnancy risk in different ways depending on dose and placement.
